Lesson 1 of 6

The Leverage Maths

Why expense reduction is one of the highest-return activities a small business can do.

The Equivalence Principle

Expense reduction and sales growth both improve net profit — but they are not equivalent in effort. Reducing an expense by $1 puts $1 directly to net profit. Generating an extra $1 of net profit through sales requires generating $1 divided by your net margin in additional revenue. For a business with a 10% net margin, every $1 of profit requires $10 in new sales. That ratio makes expense reduction extraordinarily powerful.

Expense Reduction vs Equivalent Sales Growth
Based on $1,000,000 revenue with 10% net profit margin ($100,000 net profit)
Expense ReductionAnnual SavingEquivalent Sales Growth
1% cost reduction$10,00010% sales increase
2% cost reduction$20,00020% sales increase

11% cost reduction$110,000Doubles net profit
💡 The Most Important Number in This Module

Your net profit margin determines your leverage ratio

If your net profit margin is 10%, every $1 of expense saved equals $10 in new sales. At 20% margin it is $5 in sales. At 5% margin it is $20 in sales. The lower your current margin, the more powerful expense reduction is relative to sales growth.

This Does Not Mean Stop Growing Sales

The lesson is not that sales growth is unimportant — it is that expense reduction is significantly underinvested in most small businesses relative to its return. The optimal business pursues both simultaneously, but most businesses spend 90% of their strategy time on sales and almost none on systematic cost reduction.

Lesson 2 of 6

The Systematic Expense Audit

A repeatable process for finding and eliminating cost waste in every area of your business.

  • 1

    Print your last 3 months of bank statements and P&L

    You cannot audit what you cannot see. Get every expense on paper or screen. Sort by amount — largest to smallest. The big numbers deserve the most attention but the small recurring ones often add up to significant totals.

  • 2

    Categorise every expense — necessary, negotiable, eliminate

    Three categories: (1) Necessary and correct price — leave alone. (2) Necessary but could be cheaper — schedule renegotiation. (3) No longer needed or low value — eliminate immediately. Most audits find 15–25% of expenses in category 3.

  • 3

    Focus on recurring monthly expenses first

    One-off costs are sunk. Recurring costs compound — every dollar saved per month is $12 per year. A $200/month software subscription you barely use is $2,400/year you are paying for nothing. Recurring costs deserve disproportionate attention.

  • 4

    Supplier audit — when did you last renegotiate?

    For every supplier relationship over 12 months old, the question is: are you still getting the best price? Markets change. New suppliers enter. Existing suppliers reduce rates for loyal customers who simply ask. If you have not asked in 12 months, ask now.

  • 5

    The subscription audit — the modern expense trap

    Most businesses have accumulated software subscriptions they no longer actively use. Average small business has 8–12 active SaaS subscriptions. Audit every one: when was it last used? By whom? Is there a free alternative? Is there overlap with another tool?

  • 6

    Set a monthly expense review as a recurring calendar item

    The expense audit is not a one-time exercise. Schedule 60 minutes on the first Monday of every month to review the previous month's expenses. This habit alone typically saves 5–8% annually.

Lesson 3 of 6

Supplier Management and Purchasing Discipline

How to systematically reduce your cost of goods and services through better supplier relationships.

Suppliers Respond to Systems

The businesses that pay the lowest prices for inputs are not the largest businesses — they are the most organised ones. A small business with a clear purchasing system, regular supplier reviews and a willingness to ask for better terms often achieves lower input costs than much larger competitors who treat purchasing as an administrative function.

  • 1

    Create a preferred supplier register

    Document your main suppliers, what you pay, payment terms, and the date you last reviewed pricing. This register becomes your purchasing management tool. Review it quarterly.

  • 2

    Implement annual supplier reviews

    Schedule a formal meeting with every significant supplier annually. Bring your volume data. Ask for: a loyalty discount, improved payment terms, or cost reduction ideas. Most suppliers will offer something to retain consistent volume.

  • 3

    Use payment terms as a negotiation tool

    Many suppliers offer 2–5% discounts for prompt payment or upfront payment. If you have the cash flow, paying early in exchange for a discount can be a very high-return use of cash — a 2% discount for paying 30 days early is equivalent to a 24% annual return on that cash.

  • 4

    Consolidate suppliers where possible

    Having 3 suppliers for similar items gives you less purchasing power than having 1–2 who get all your volume. Consolidation often unlocks volume discounts that more than compensate for the loss of having multiple options.

  • 5

    Train everyone who makes purchases

    If staff members make purchasing decisions, they should understand the business's pricing benchmarks and the expectation to always seek competitive pricing. A staff member who buys from the most convenient supplier rather than the most competitive one is costing you money every time.

💡 The Ask Is Free

How much do you save if you never ask? Zero.

The single most underused profit improvement strategy is simply asking your existing suppliers for a better deal. The worst they can say is no. Most will offer something — a small discount, better terms, a rebate for volume. Schedule supplier review conversations for the next 90 days with every supplier representing more than $500/month of spend. The average improvement from these conversations is 5–12%.

Lesson 4 of 6

Building a Cost-Conscious Culture

How to get your team thinking about cost efficiency without making them feel like they're always being watched.

Culture Drives Behaviour

The most powerful cost reduction happens when every person in your business automatically considers cost efficiency in their daily decisions — without being told to. Building this culture requires transparency about numbers, clear systems, recognition for cost-saving behaviour, and leadership that models the behaviour it wants to see.

  • 1

    Share relevant financial information with staff

    You don't need to share everything. But staff who know the gross margin, the monthly expense budget, and the targets are far more cost-conscious than those who work in financial darkness. Monthly team meetings with a simple financial dashboard change behaviour significantly.

  • 2

    Make the connection between individual actions and profit visible

    Show your team specific examples: "When we have to redo a job, it costs us $X. When we waste Y units of material, it costs $Z. When we respond to a client complaint quickly and fix it, we retain $W in annual revenue." Concrete numbers change behaviour more than general requests to "be careful."

  • 3

    Create a cost-saving suggestion system

    Ask staff: where do you see waste in your work? What takes longer than it should? What materials get wasted? Staff on the front line often see cost improvement opportunities that management never notices. Reward suggestions that get implemented.

  • 4

    Set expense budgets for each team member who spends

    Anyone who spends business money should have a clear budget and understand they are accountable to it. Monthly review: budget vs actual, variance explanation, action if over. Accountability without information is unfair — accountability with clear information and budget is normal good management.

  • 5

    Lead by example — model cost consciousness

    If you as the owner spend lavishly on things of questionable business value, your team will not take cost management seriously. The most effective cost-reduction leaders are visibly thoughtful about business spending and explain their decisions.

💡 The Monthly Financial Dashboard

One page, shared monthly, changes everything

Build a one-page monthly dashboard showing: revenue vs target, gross margin %, total expenses vs budget, and net profit. Share it with your team in a 15-minute monthly meeting. Answer questions honestly. Within 3 months you will notice a measurable change in how staff think about spending, waste and efficiency.
