Why Discounting is Dangerous

The hidden maths that most business owners have never seen.

The Discounting Illusion

When a client asks for a 10% discount, most business owners think: I need to sell 10% more to compensate. That is almost never true. The actual extra sales required to recover a discount depends on your gross margin — and for most businesses, it is shockingly higher than 10%. Understanding this maths is one of the most important financial lessons a business owner can learn.

The Discounting Impact Table

50% margin+25% more sales+43% more sales+67% more sales
40% margin+33% more sales+60% more sales+100% more sales
30% margin+50% more sales+100% more sales+200% more sales

⚠️ The 30% Margin Reality

If your gross margin is 30% and you give a 20% discount, you need to DOUBLE your sales volume just to make the same profit as before the discount. Does doubling your volume seem realistic? For most businesses the answer is no — which means every 20% discount at 30% margin destroys profit permanently.

💡 Why This Happens

The discount comes entirely out of your margin, not your costs

When you discount 10%, your costs don't change. Your revenue drops. Every dollar of discount comes directly from profit. That's why the recovery rate is so much higher than the discount rate — you're trying to recover pure profit loss through additional volume.

Lesson 3 of 6

Why Clients Ask for Discounts — and What They Really Mean

Understanding the psychology behind price objections so you can respond confidently.

The Discount Request is Usually a Test

Most clients who ask for a discount are not genuinely unable to afford your price. They are testing whether you believe in your own value. When you immediately offer a discount, you signal two things: my price was wrong to begin with, and I don't have confidence in my value. Both signals undermine the relationship before it starts.

  • 1

    Understand what "it's too expensive" actually means

    In most cases it means one of three things: (1) I don't yet see enough value to justify the price, (2) I'm testing whether this is your real price, (3) I genuinely have a budget constraint. Each requires a different response. Discounting solves only the third case — and only if it's genuine.

  • 2

    Respond with value, not a lower price

    When a client says the price is too high, ask: "What would need to be different for this to be worth that investment to you?" This forces a conversation about value rather than price, and often reveals what they actually need to hear.

  • 3

    Offer a scope reduction rather than a discount

    Instead of reducing price for the same work, offer to remove something from the scope. "I can't reduce the price on this package, but I can do a smaller scope for $X if that works better for your budget." This maintains your price integrity while giving the client options.

  • 4

    Have a genuine no-discount policy and explain it

    Many businesses successfully operate with a no-discount policy. The key is communicating it with confidence: "We don't discount because our pricing reflects the quality of work we deliver and we don't want to compromise that for any client." Confidence in your position is persuasive.

  • 5

    Know your walk-away number

    Before every proposal, decide the minimum you will accept. If a client asks for more discount than your walk-away, you politely decline and move on. Not every client is the right client — and a client you won by deep discounting is often more trouble than they're worth.

📍 The Confident Response

A script for the next time a client asks for a discount

Client: "Can you do it for less?"

Response: "I appreciate you asking. Our pricing reflects [specific value element — e.g. the monthly strategy calls, the specialised industry knowledge, the guaranteed response time]. I'm not able to reduce the price on this package, but what I can do is [offer a smaller scope alternative or explain the payment terms]. Would either of those work better for you?"

This response does not apologise, does not immediately discount, and gives the client a genuine alternative.

Lesson 4 of 6

Building a Pricing Defence System

The systems and policies that protect your prices without needing to have the same conversation every time.

Systematise Your Price Defence

Ad hoc responses to discount requests produce inconsistent results. Building a systematic approach to pricing defence means your response is always confident, always consistent and always in the best interest of your business margin.

  • 1

    Create a formal pricing policy

    Write it down. Your pricing policy should state: standard prices, what is and is not negotiable, payment terms, and the conditions under which you will offer a reduced scope. Having it written makes it real — for you and your staff.

  • 2

    Build value into your proposal before price appears

    By the time the client sees your price, they should already understand exactly what they are getting and why it is worth it. Lead with outcomes and ROI. Show the problem, show your solution, show the result — then show the price. Price at the end of a strong value story lands very differently than price at the top of a quote.

  • 3

    Use annual price increases as a discipline

    Build a 3–5% annual price increase into your business calendar. Communicate it professionally and in advance. Clients who push back on a small annual increase are telling you something important about the relationship.

  • 4

    Train staff who quote to never discount without approval

    If you have staff or contractors who provide quotes, they must not have authority to discount without management approval. A 10% discount given casually by a staff member costs the same as one given by the owner — the business doesn't know the difference.

  • 5

    Track your average sale value monthly

    If your average sale value starts declining, it is often an early warning of discounting creep — the gradual normalisation of discounting across the business. Tracking it monthly catches this early.

💡 The Annual Price Increase Letter

A simple template that protects margin every year

Dear [Client],

Thank you for your continued support of [Business Name]. As of [date], our pricing will increase by [X]% to reflect rising costs and continued investment in the quality of our service.

Your new [service] price will be [new price] per month. Your current pricing will remain in place until [date].

We value your business and remain committed to delivering [key value proposition].

This simple annual communication, sent 60 days before the increase takes effect, typically results in 85–90% of clients accepting the increase without question.
