Profit is Not Just About Sales

The five levers of profit — and why most businesses only pull one of them.

The Five Profit Levers

Most small business owners focus almost exclusively on growing sales when they want to improve profit. But there are five distinct levers that drive profit — and in many cases the non-sales levers deliver faster results with less effort. Understanding all five and having a strategy for each one is the foundation of serious profit improvement.

1
Lever One
Increase Sales Revenue
More clients, higher prices, more transactions per client.
2
Lever Two
Improve Gross Margin
Better purchasing, less waste, stronger pricing discipline.
3
Lever Three
Reduce Operating Expenses
Supplier renegotiation, waste elimination, smarter spending.
4
Lever Four
Improve Productivity
More output per hour, better systems, less rework.
5
Lever Five
Protect Against Margin Erosion
Stop discounting, maintain prices, train staff on margin impact.
💡 The Most Overlooked Lever

Expense reduction is faster than sales growth

Growing sales takes time — marketing, leads, conversion, delivery. Cutting $10,000 in expenses puts $10,000 straight to your bottom line immediately. For a business with a 10% net profit margin, a $10,000 expense reduction is equivalent to winning $100,000 in new sales. That ratio changes how you think about cost control.

Lesson 2 of 6

Purchasing and Cost of Sales Strategies

How to improve your gross margin through smarter buying, not just more selling.

Gross Margin is Where Profit Starts

Before you can control your bottom line you need to understand your gross margin — the difference between what you charge and what it costs to deliver your product or service. Every percentage point of gross margin improvement flows directly to net profit. A business doing $500k revenue with a 40% gross margin has $200k to cover expenses and profit. At 45% gross margin that's $225k — an extra $25,000 without a single new client.

  • 1

    Implement a 3-quote system for all major purchases

    For any purchase over a set threshold — say $500 — require 3 quotes minimum. This single discipline typically saves 10–20% on purchasing costs and makes suppliers more competitive on price.

  • 2

    Negotiate 12-month buying agreements

    If you buy regularly from suppliers, negotiate annual agreements with fixed pricing, volume discounts and payment terms. Suppliers will give significant discounts for guaranteed volume and prompt payment.

  • 3

    Benchmark your supplier pricing quarterly

    Create a simple comparison register of your main purchases across multiple suppliers. Review it every quarter. Markets change — the cheapest supplier 12 months ago may not be cheapest today.

  • 4

    Ask suppliers directly for cost reduction ideas

    Your suppliers know their products better than you do. Ask them: "What could we do differently to reduce our costs?" You will often get genuinely useful suggestions — bulk ordering, different specifications, timing of orders.

  • 5

    Reduce waste systematically

    Track waste in your production or delivery process. Even service businesses have waste — time on non-billable work, rework, admin inefficiency. Every dollar of waste eliminated improves gross margin.

  • 6

    Review your pricing against cost increases

    Many small businesses absorb supplier cost increases rather than passing them on. If your costs have risen 8% in the past 12 months and your prices haven't moved, your gross margin has compressed by the same amount.

📍 Brisbane Example — Trades Business

A plumbing company saved $38,000 per year on materials

They implemented a 3-quote system for materials, negotiated a 12-month agreement with their primary supplier (8% discount for guaranteed volume), and started tracking material waste per job. Result: gross margin improved from 42% to 49% without a single additional client.

Lesson 3 of 6

Operating Expense Reduction

The specific expense categories where most small businesses are overspending — and exactly what to do about it.

The Expense Audit

Most small business owners have not reviewed every line of their operating expenses in the past 12 months. Market rates change, usage patterns change, better options emerge. A systematic expense audit typically finds 5–15% in savings without cutting anything essential. Start with your bank and credit card statements — go through every recurring charge and ask: is this still delivering value? Could I get it cheaper?

📦 Supplier Costs

Negotiate 12-month agreements. Get 3 quotes on major purchases. Ask for early payment discounts. Reduce stock holdings — cash tied up in stock is expensive.

📱 IT and Telecommunications

Review mobile plans annually — the market changes constantly. Audit software subscriptions — you likely have 3–4 you barely use. Negotiate internet contracts every 24 months.

📢 Marketing and Advertising

Measure every channel. Cut anything with no measurable return. Buy advertising at discounted rates (end of month, remnant space). Shift budget to email and referrals — highest ROI, lowest cost.

🏢 Office and Administration

Appoint one person responsible for office purchasing. Review insurance policies annually — shop around at renewal. Eliminate subscriptions nobody uses. Use private-label supplies where quality is equivalent.

Staff Cost Efficiency

Labour is typically the largest operating expense. This is not about reducing staff — it is about maximising the value of every hour paid. Clear job descriptions, performance targets, training to reduce errors and rework, and systems that eliminate manual tasks all improve profit per employee without reducing headcount.

💡 The Expense Audit Process

90 minutes that could save you $20,000+

Step 1: Print last 3 months of bank and credit card statements. Step 2: Highlight every recurring charge. Step 3: For each one ask — do we still need this? Is there a cheaper alternative? Has usage changed? Step 4: Cancel anything that fails the test. Step 5: Renegotiate everything else. Most business owners find 8–12 items they can cut or reduce immediately.

Lesson 4 of 6

People Strategies and Productivity

How to get more profit from your existing team without working more hours.

Profit Per Employee

The most powerful productivity metric for a small business is profit per employee — total net profit divided by number of staff including yourself. If your business makes $80,000 net profit with 4 staff that's $20,000 per employee. Tracking this annually tells you whether adding staff is actually improving profitability or just adding cost.

  • 1

    Train staff on the financial impact of their decisions

    Most employees have no idea what their actions cost the business. A tradesperson who wastes $50 of materials per job, across 5 jobs per day and 220 working days, wastes $55,000 per year. Show your team these numbers. Financial literacy in staff is one of the most underrated profit improvement tools.

  • 2

    Implement clear performance standards

    Vague expectations produce average results. Every role should have 3–5 clear, measurable performance standards. Not "do a good job" but "complete 8 service calls per day with a customer satisfaction score above 4.5."

  • 3

    Eliminate rework — track it as a cost

    Every time a job needs to be redone, that is pure cost with zero revenue. Track rework incidents. Find the root cause. Build quality checks into the process before the cost is incurred, not after.

  • 4

    Reward performance that improves profit

    Commission, bonuses and incentives aligned to profit — not just revenue — create a team that thinks like owners. A salesperson paid only on revenue will discount. A salesperson paid on gross margin will protect it.

  • 5

    Build systems that reduce owner dependency

    Every time you are the only person who can do something, your business has a bottleneck that limits growth and profit. Document processes. Train others. The goal is a business that runs well without you for at least 2 weeks.

💡 The Staff Profit Conversation

Have you ever shown your team your gross margin?

Most business owners never share financial information with staff. But employees who understand that the business operates on a 40% gross margin and that every $100 of waste costs $100 of profit — not just $100 of revenue — make completely different decisions. You don't need to share net profit. But sharing gross margin and the cost of waste changes behaviour immediately.
