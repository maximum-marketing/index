Lesson 1 of 6

Why Risk Management Is Not Optional

The businesses that survive unexpected events are the ones that thought about them before they happened.

The Domino Effect of Unmanaged Risk

Businesses rarely fail from a single catastrophic event. They fail because one problem triggers another which triggers another -- a domino effect that an unprepared business cannot stop. A key staff member leaves and takes client relationships with them. A key client cancels and cash flow collapses. A data breach destroys client trust and triggers legal liability. Each individual risk is manageable. The cascade is what destroys businesses. Risk management is the discipline of identifying these dominoes before they fall.

The Six Risk Categories Every Small Business Faces
Financial

Cash flow failure, client concentration, bad debts, unexpected costs, economic downturn

Operational

Key supplier failure, system outages, process breakdowns, quality failures, delivery issues

People

Key staff resignation, owner incapacity, workplace injury, skill shortages, team conflict

Technology

Cyberattack, data breach, system failure, software obsolescence, data loss

Reputational

Negative reviews, social media crisis, client complaint going public, ethical breach

External

Natural disaster, regulatory change, new competitor, economic shock, pandemic-type disruption

The Business That Plans vs The Business That Reacts

Planning for risk does not mean expecting disaster -- it means being prepared for probability

You do not need to plan for every possible risk. You need to plan for the most likely risks with the highest potential impact. A well-run small business with 10 staff in Brisbane is not primarily worried about a hurricane. It is worried about losing its key client, its key staff member, its data, and its cash flow. Risk management is about being honest about your specific vulnerabilities.

Lesson 2 of 6

Identifying and Categorising Your Risks

How to systematically find the specific events that could disrupt your business.

Move from Abstract to Specific

Most business owners acknowledge they have risks in a vague general way. Risk management requires moving from "I might lose a key client" (abstract) to "Client A represents 32% of my revenue and their contract expires in 4 months with no renewal discussion yet" (specific). Specific risks can be acted on. Abstract ones sit in the back of your mind without resolution.

Risk Identification Examples -- Small Service Business
Financial

Client concentration risk

Top 2 clients represent 55% of revenue. Loss of either would create immediate cash flow crisis and require emergency acquisition efforts.

Financial

Cash flow gap

Invoice payment terms of 30 days means payroll must be funded from reserves. If 3+ clients pay late simultaneously, payroll is at risk.

Operational

Owner dependency

Most technical delivery knowledge resides with the owner. No documented processes. If owner is incapacitated, delivery quality collapses immediately.

Operational

Key supplier failure

Business relies on one SEO software platform. If that platform fails or significantly increases pricing, delivery model is disrupted without a tested alternative.

People

Key staff resignation

Account manager has client relationships and system knowledge. Departure would damage client retention and require 3-4 months to replace effectively.

Technology

Data breach or cyberattack

Client data stored on local systems with basic password protection. A breach would create legal liability, reputational damage and potential loss of multiple contracts.

Reputational

Negative reviews campaign

A disgruntled former client or competitor could systematically post negative Google reviews. No monitoring system or response protocol in place.

The Risk Brainstorm Process

Set aside 90 minutes and go through each of the six categories systematically

For each category ask: what specific event could occur? What would trigger it? How would it actually play out in your business? Write everything down without filtering. You can assess and prioritise later. The goal of the brainstorm is completeness -- the assessment process will help you decide which risks are worth planning for.

Business Impact Analysis -- Assessing What Each Risk Really Costs

Not all risks are equal. This lesson shows you how to rank them so you invest your planning time where it matters most.

Three Dimensions of Impact

Every risk has three distinct impact dimensions: financial, people, and operational. A risk might have catastrophic financial impact but minimal people impact. Another might have moderate financial impact but severe reputational and operational consequences. Understanding all three dimensions gives you a complete picture of the true cost of each risk -- and helps you prioritise your mitigation investment correctly.

The Priority Formula

High impact + High likelihood = Immediate action. High impact + Low likelihood = Contingency plan. Low impact + Any likelihood = Monitor only.

This simple matrix prevents two common mistakes: ignoring high-probability medium-impact risks because they seem manageable, and over-investing in dramatic but extremely unlikely events. Focus your planning effort on risks that are both probable and serious. For everything else, a simple contingency note is sufficient.

Lesson 4 of 6

Risk Mitigation -- Building Your Response Plans

The difference between a risk you have planned for and one that catches you by surprise is enormous.

Mitigation vs Contingency

Risk management has two modes: mitigation (reducing the likelihood of the risk occurring) and contingency (reducing the impact when it does occur). Both are essential. Mitigation might mean diversifying your client base so no single client represents more than 20% of revenue. Contingency planning means having a documented process for acquiring emergency replacement revenue if a major client does cancel. You need both because mitigation alone cannot eliminate all risk.

  • 1

    Step 1 -- Identify current controls

    For each high-priority risk, honestly assess what you currently have in place. Not what you intend to have -- what actually exists today. Most businesses have far fewer controls than they think.

  • 2

    Step 2 -- Define the mitigation action

    For each risk, identify one specific action that would meaningfully reduce either the likelihood or the impact. Be specific. "Improve cybersecurity" is not a mitigation action. "Enable two-factor authentication on all systems and encrypt the client data folder by [date]" is a mitigation action.

  • 3

    Step 3 -- Define the contingency response

    If this risk occurs despite mitigation, what do you do? Who does what? In what order? A one-page contingency script for each critical risk is all you need. It does not need to be comprehensive -- it needs to be available when you are under stress and cannot think clearly.

  • 4

    Step 4 -- Assign ownership and a deadline

    Every mitigation action needs one named owner and a specific deadline. Shared ownership means no ownership. "Someone will look at this" means it will never happen.

  • 5

    Step 5 -- Review quarterly

    Risks change. Your business changes. New risks emerge. Review your risk plan quarterly alongside your KPI scorecard. 30 minutes four times a year is all it takes to maintain a relevant, actionable risk plan.

Mitigation Plan Example -- Key Staff Resignation
Current Controls

None. No documented processes. No succession plan. No client handover protocol.

Mitigation Actions

1. Document all client management processes this quarter (owner responsibility, by [date])
2. Begin monthly career development conversations with key staff (starts next Monday)
3. Implement competitive salary review annually (HR calendar item)

Contingency Response (if it happens)

Week 1: Owner directly manages all affected clients. Email all clients proactively. Week 2: Post job ad and begin recruitment. Month 2: Shortlist and interview. Month 3: New person starts with 2-week handover period with departing staff if possible.
